Exercise 1 (Ecology & Biology)– Fish Population Reintroduction (4 points)

A local environmental agency reintroduced a vulnerable native fish species into a closed lake basin. Biologists monitored the population size N(t) weekly over a year.

  1. Given that the underlying carrying capacity of the lake is strictly bounded by space and food, write down the analytical solution of the Logistic growth model.

The analytical solution in such a case is given by N(t)=K/(1+((K-N0)/N0)exp(-rt)) where N0 is the initial population, K is the carrying capacity and r is the growth rate.

  1. Using the provided simulated data, estimate the intrinsic growth rate r and the initial population N0 using non-linear least squares (nls in R), assuming a fixed carrying capacity K=1000.

Exercise 3 (Energy & Natural Resources)– Oil Reservoir Peak Exploitation (4 points)

A mature national offshore oil basin undergoes conventional extraction. Geologists need to map the historical flow rate P(t) to project the absolute peak of extraction and total Ultimate Recoverable Resources (URR).

  1. Express the Hubbert Curve formula as the mathematical time derivative of a cumulative logistic trend.

The cumulative logistic curve can be rewriten, after a change of parameters, as

\(f(t)=\frac{k}{\eta+\alpha^t}\)

for \(k>0\) and \(0<\alpha<1\). Then, the Hubbert production flow (curve) is the instantaneous production rate of the previous cumulative logistic trend, that is, the time derivative

\(P(t)=x_0(\frac{\eta+\alpha^{t_0}}{\eta+\alpha^t})^2 \alpha^{t-t_0}\), where \((t_0, x_0)\) is an initial observation.

Note that t appears as exponent of \(\alpha\) both in the numerator and denominator of the expression.

  1. Fit the Hubbert production flow model via non-linear least squares to the historical extraction data. Extract the estimated parameters for the peak (x(tmax)) , peak extraction year (tmax), exploitation speed (r), and total available resource volume (URR).
# Ex 3: Hubbert Production Flow

hub_clean <- x0_hub*(((eta_hub + alpha_hub^t0_hub)/(eta_hub + alpha_hub^t_seq))^2)*alpha_hub^(t_seq - t0_hub)
hub_data <- data.frame(time = t_seq, P = hub_clean + rnorm(length(t_seq), mean=0, sd=27))
plot(t_seq, hub_data$P)
lines(t_seq, hub_clean, lwd = 2)

fit_hub <- nls(P ~ x0_hub*(((eta + alpha^t0_hub)/(eta + alpha^time))^2)*alpha^(time - t0_hub), data = hub_data, start = list(eta = 0, alpha = 0.1))

Exercise 4 (Epidemiology)– Viral Outbreak Kinematics (3 points)

During the initial stage of a high-density clinical culture viral infection outbreak, the daily cumulative infected count N(t) expands rapidly without clear immediate resource limitations.

  1. Formulate the Malthusian ordinary differential equation and explain why its application must be strictly restricted to early epidemic outbreaks.

EDO: \(\frac{dN(t)}{dt}=rN(t)\)

Note that the solution is an exponential which, for \(r>0\), increases indefinitely as time goes to infinity. This assumes infinite resources, which theoretically cause an unbounded exponential growth of the population, which is not a feasible situation.

Thus, this model needs to be applied to early epidemic outbreaks, when there are a lot of resources and there exists an initial unconstrained growth phase.

  1. Transform the exponential model N(t) = N0exp(rt) into a linear log-transformed framework (ln N(t) = β0 + β1t). Use the linear least squares approach (lm in R) to estimate the baseline parameter N0 and the growth speed r.

y=log(N)=log(N0)+rt. Thus, N0=exp(beta0), r=beta1.

# Ex 4: Exponential Viral Outbreak (Early stage truncated to t=20)
t_early <- seq(0, 18, by = 1)
N0_vir <- 80; r_vir <- 0.18
viral_clean <- N0_vir * exp(r_vir * t_early)
viral_data <- data.frame(time = t_early, N = viral_clean + rnorm(length(t_early), mean=0, sd=10))
fit_lm <- lm(log(N) ~ time, data = viral_data)
params <- coef(fit_lm)

beta0 <- params["(Intercept)"]
beta1 <- params["time"]
(N0=exp(beta0))

(r=beta1)

  1. Identify at which point or capacity threshold the deterministic exponential path dramatically fails when compared to a realistic saturated epidemic profile.

The deterministic exponential growth model begins to fail as the population approaches the carrying capacity K, when resource limitations become significant and growth slows down. In contrast, when the population size is much smaller than K, the logistic model behaves approximately like exponential growth, since resource constraints are negligible and the term (1 − N/K) is close to 1. This can be seen directly from the logistic differential equation. As N approaches K, the factor (1 − N/K) approaches zero, causing the growth rate to decline and the exponential approximation to fail.
